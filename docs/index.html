A Mouse's Journey
How does a female mice's estrus cycle affect its daily activity and temperature compared to male mice?

How we got here:

The fundamental idea behind our project was to showcase trends and differences between male and female mice that may be obscured by static graphs. Experiments our group chose to answer included finding differences in the distribution of body temperatures between males and females, as well as determining which class of mice showed greater activity levels. Our website works with daily activity and body temperature data of male and female mice during a 14 day study. Day and night were simulated using artificial lighting on 12-hour intervals. Estrus cycles for female mice were also recorded, starting on the second day and occurring every 4 days. This project allows users to observe distributions of activity levels and body temperatures for male and female mice, the latter of which can be further analyzed by isolating trends during estrus. Through the use of easy-to-read visuals, the goal of our project is to create an interactive tool that best reports the trends and differences of mice in simulated environments while the facilitating user experience.

Design Rationale:

In order to effectively compare distributions across groups, we used a histogram to represent the data, where the x-axis represents either temperature or activity and the y-axis indicates data density. This allows us to distinguish between males and females using a colorblind-friendly palette (blue for male and orange for female). We decided to use a histogram because it allows us to quickly compare distributions between both genders, making patterns and group differences easier to discern. For the estrus state, we decided to use a pinkish-red highlight to show what the density of the data would look like during the cylce. Alternatively, we were thinking about a time-based exploration view, where the visual encodings shift slightly to capture temporal patterns. For this type of visualization, the x-axis encodes time throughout the day, allowing users to observe variations in temperature or activity. The y-axis would instead represent the chosen measure—temperature or activity—and the same color encoding is maintained for group association to keep the visualization consistent and intuitive. A shaded region would have been used to highlight the estrus state in female mice. This shading acts as a visual cue, emphasizing the specific periods when estrus occurs, thereby helping to correlate these phases with changes in temperature or activity. Together, these visual encodings create a layered narrative that not only shows overall distribution differences but also reveals how time is correlated to biological states within the data. We did not go for this type of visualization because the visualization would have been heavily cluttered, making it difficult to discern patterns within the data. We understand that there are tradeoffs for our type of visualization such as losing the ability to pan over the entire graph minute by minute, but this allows us to have a clearer comparison of how the temperature/activity are distributed across all sexes and estrus states. To enhance user engagement, we incorporated several interactive techniques. Users can select which measure to display—temperature or activity—using a simple dropdown or button toggle. Dynamic query filters allow for refining the data by gender, estrus state. We chose a colorblind-friendly palette with distinct hues for each group and a subtle overlay for estrus periods, ensuring that the visual encoding remained both accessible and effective.

Development Process:

Our development process was highly collaborative. Initially, all team members engaged in exploratory data analysis using Python libraries such as pandas and seaborn to understand the data’s structure, identify key trends, and how the estrus period affects these trends. We used a series of plots using matplotlib and seaborn to understand these relationships. This analysis led us to use distribution charts as a primary means of comparison. We proceeded with data preparation by converting the dataset into a long format, computing time-of-day values, and merging estrus state data into each record. The wide-to-long conversion pivoted the temperature and activity data so that D3 could easily bind the data to visual elements. During the implementation phase, we built the visualization using HTML, CSS, and D3.js, setting up SVG elements, formatting the web page, and integrating interactive features such as dynamic filtering.

Division of Labor:

Each team member conducted an initial EDA of the dataset. Each team member created their own set of visualizations after exploring the dataset and presented it to the team. After this process, we designed a final idea and went with it. Dallas and Edgar focused on the D3 visualization, while Banff and Manjot focused on styling and formatting the web page. The pairs would switch off every couple of hours and this allowed us to stay fresh and give a fresh set of eyes on each other's problems. Then we would partner-program. This took a total of 35-40 hours give or take with a majority of the time spent on the webpage and D3 visualizations itself. The hardest part was the D3 visualization and making sure the filtering drop down boxes were aligned and working. This did take a majority of our time, but what took the most was our data preparation. Preparing the data for use was crucial because if we did not have the data the visualization would not have made sense.
